1. Do you think this current system provides adequate accountability around short-term
rentals in San Francisco? In your response, describe why you believe the system is
adequate or inadequate. If you believe the system is inadequate, discuss a possible
change in actions required to improve accountability in this system. You may refer to the
SWE Code of Ethics as you think through your response.

I don't think the current system for regulating short-term rentals in
San Francisco is adequate. The biggest issue I see is that the
government only investigates listings after someone files a complaint.
That puts all the responsibility on regular people or independent
investigators to catch hosts who aren't following the rules rather than
the platform or agency. Most people browsing Airbnb would not know how
to identify an invalid policy number or recognize when a host is
operating without proper registration. This means violations can persist
for extended periods before anyone notices or takes action. Principle 1
of the Software Engineering Code of Ethics states that software
engineers should act in the public interest. In my opinion, the current
system fails this standard because it doesn't use technology to protect
the public from housing shortages caused by unlicensed short-term
rentals. A more effective approach would require Airbnb to verify policy
numbers against the city's official database before allowing a listing
to be published. This would shift accountability to the platform, which
has the technical capability to automate compliance checks, rather than
expecting a third party to perform investigative work.

2. As discussed in the introduction, the legality of web scraping is still uncertain in the US.
Skim through the Legal Issues section of Web Scraping in the US on Wikipedia and this
article about the legal issues with the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act, and describe at
least one factor you believe is important to consider when discussing the legality of web
scraping and why.

One important factor to consider when discussing the legality of web
scraping is the distinction between accessing publicly available
information and bypassing technical barriers intended to restrict
access. After reviewing the Wikipedia article on web scraping legality
and the article about the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act, I found that the
law remains unclear on this issue. The CFAA uses language about
"exceeding authorized access," but this becomes difficult to interpret
when the data in question is visible to anyone who visits a webpage
without logging in or bypassing any security measures. This distinction
matters because it determines whether scraping constitutes unauthorized
access or simply collecting information that is already publicly
displayed. If violating a website's Terms of Service were enough to
trigger legal consequences, companies could effectively prevent any
outside scrutiny of their platforms, even for purposes that serve the
public interest. For accountability projects like this one, where the
goal is to verify compliance with housing regulations, the legal
uncertainty creates challenges. I believe the law should more clearly
protect scraping of genuinely public data while still prohibiting
activities that involve hacking or causing harm to a website's
operations.

3. Scraping public data does not always lead to positive results for society. For example,
look to Facebook–Cambridge Analytica data scandal - Wikipedia or Clearview AI -
Wikipedia. Many argue that using someone’s personal data without their consent (even if
publicly provided) is unethical. Web scraping requires thoughtful intervention. Develop at
least two guidelines you think are important to consider when deciding whether to do
web scraping.

The first guideline I think is important to consider when deciding
whether to do web scraping is to collect only the data that is actually
necessary for your purpose. Even when information is publicly
accessible, individuals may not expect that data to be collected and
analyzed at a large scale. The Cambridge Analytica scandal is a good
example of this problem. Users shared information on Facebook in a
social context, not anticipating that it would be harvested and used for
political targeting. For any scraping project, the question of what is
the minimum amount of data required to achieve a legitimate purpose
should be asked. The second guideline I think is important is to
consider the potential for misuse before collecting any data. The
Clearview AI case demonstrates the dangers of large-scale data
collection without adequate consideration of consequences. By scraping
billions of publicly posted photos to build a facial recognition
database, the company created a tool that can be used for surveillance
and tracking in ways that the people in those photos never consented to.
Before beginning a scraping project, it is worth asking what harm could
result if the data were leaked or used for something other than its
original intent. If the potential for misuse is significant, additional
safeguards should be implemented, or the project should be reconsidered
entirely.
